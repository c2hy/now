When I was in college, I once spent a school break working at an electronics mall.

It was a shop that sold laptops and tablets. I had no sales experience, so most of the time I just greeted customers or helped them with simple computer problems. When a product actually needed selling, the owner usually stepped in himself.

He clearly knew a lot more than I did.

He knew the models, configurations, and specifications of all kinds of computers. Whenever a customer came in, he could talk at length about them. I listened from the side and sometimes thought I should take notes. Maybe I would need to know these things someday.

But once, I had a strange feeling.

The owner was recommending a tablet to a customer. As usual, he started listing all the machine's advantages. One term stuck with me: IPS panel.

I can't remember exactly what he said. I only remember a string of technical terms that sounded very professional. He spoke as if none of them needed explaining. Standing beside him, I could barely understand a thing.

By all rights, I should have been impressed.

After all, I was just a college student working part time. I didn't even know what an IPS panel was, while he spent every day around these products. He was obviously more qualified than I was to tell people what made a tablet good.

But the thought that came to mind was:

If I were the customer, I wouldn't want to hear any of this.

That thought confused me, too.

What made someone who knew nothing about sales think there was a problem with the owner's approach? Maybe customers liked hearing these technical explanations. Maybe they were exactly what showed how well he knew his products.

I didn't know.

Still, if I walked into an electronics mall planning to spend a fair amount of money on a tablet, I doubt the first thing I would want to know was what kind of panel it used.

I might just want to know what it was like to use, whether the screen felt comfortable to look at, and whether I would regret buying it.

Of course, technical specifications might eventually answer those questions. But why should I have to learn the specifications first to know whether a computer was right for me?

During the time I worked there, the shop wasn't doing very well.

I don't know whether that had anything to do with the owner's way of selling. Business in an electronics mall depends on too many things. Maybe the shop was in a bad location. Maybe its prices weren't competitive. Maybe the whole market was slow.

Yet many years later, I still remember that explanation of IPS panels.

Not because I eventually learned what IPS was, but because I gradually realized that the owner's behavior made a lot of sense.

He worked with these products every day. He needed to know the differences between models, the strengths and weaknesses of each configuration, and why one computer cost more than another. This knowledge mattered to him. It was how he made a living.

Of course he would come to know them better and better.

Maybe that's where the problem began.

When you study something every day, you gradually get used to understanding it in the same way you study it.

To someone familiar with computers, an IPS panel isn't an unfamiliar technical term. It points to a set of specific display characteristics, and to certain advantages this product has over another.

Those connections were already in the owner's mind. He didn't even have to think them through anymore.

But the customers standing in front of him hadn't gone through that process.

The owner said one term, and a whole set of judgments about the product came to mind. The customer may have heard only the term itself.

It seemed there was a distance between them.

The more the owner knew, the harder that distance might have been for him to notice.

Later, I started making my own products.

At first, I just wanted to solve problems I had run into myself. But as development went on, I had more and more things to think about: how to design features, how to organize data, why to use one implementation over another, and how my product differed from others on the market.

All of that mattered to me.

I spent a lot of time on small details, sometimes just to make an experience that seemed insignificant a little better. Naturally, I wanted other people to understand that effort.

But now, when I try to introduce my products to someone, I sometimes think of that owner in the electronics mall.

Because I've realized that I have a lot to say, too.

I want to explain my design decisions, why a feature is different, and why I think this approach is better than the usual one.

The person listening may never have thought about any of those questions.

I spent months figuring this out. Can I really expect someone else to understand it in a few minutes?

Worse, the more I learn about my product, the easier it is to forget why I started making it in the first place.

Back then, I didn't know what technology to use. I didn't know which competing products were on the market or what supposedly correct product design looked like.

I had simply run into a problem.

A problem simple enough to explain without any technical terms.

Later, I learned more and more, until I could finally build a product that solved it. But I also grew used to explaining the product with all that knowledge.

It was only then that I realized the college student standing in that electronics mall might have had something I am gradually losing now.

He knew nothing.

So when someone described a tablet to him, he wanted to know just one thing:

**What's in it for me?**

Now I finally know a lot more.

I have to be careful not to become the person explaining IPS panels to him.
