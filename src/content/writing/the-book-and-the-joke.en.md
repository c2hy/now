For a while, the internet made Yu Hua look like a comedian who had somehow ended up writing novels.

He cracked jokes, complained about everyday life, and said ridiculous things with a straight face. People made memes out of him and gave him nicknames. For some, his sense of humor was the first thing that got them curious about his books.

That's a little strange when you think about it.

_To Live_ came out more than thirty years ago. Yu Hua had been a good writer all along. His books were already there. Short videos hadn't added any chapters or made the prose any better.

What changed in the last few years was how people got interested in him.

It used to be that you read one of his books and found out who Yu Hua was.

Now you might find him funny first and then wonder what he's written.

I keep coming back to a question about that.

**Why does something that's already good sometimes need something that seems much less important to help more people find it?**

## A picture that barely fit

A while ago, I tried a small experiment.

I paired a social media post with a picture that had little to do with the text. I just thought the picture was interesting.

The post reached more people than I expected.

It reminded me of a kind of video you see on Douyin. Someone is telling a story, and next to them there's footage of a parkour game, someone slicing soap, or something else that's hard to look away from.

The story and the footage may have nothing in common. People watch anyway.

My first thought was that maybe I'd found a way to game the recommendation algorithm.

Platforms want people to linger. If an image gets people to stop for longer, the algorithm might take that as a sign that the whole post is worth showing to more people.

That sounded reasonable.

The more I thought about it, the more presumptuous it seemed.

**What if people genuinely liked the pairing?**

Imagine reading a very serious paragraph with a picture of a cat making a ridiculous face underneath it.

The cat probably won't help you grasp some profound idea.

It might make the paragraph funny, though.

Swap out the picture and the same words can go from earnest to sarcastic, or from sad to absurd.

The image adds little information. It changes how the words feel when you read them.

On the internet, part of what we come for is the information itself.

Part of it is how someone chose to present it.

People have studied this. An unexpected contrast between an image and its text can sometimes get people to engage more. A caption can also make an image more interesting by giving it a meaning it wouldn't have on its own.

Of course, you can't just stick any random picture on a post and expect it to work.

What matters may be whether the two are interesting together. How closely they relate is a separate question.

That made me rethink something else.

## Does the internet really reward good work?

People often say that if your work is good enough, somebody will find it eventually.

I used to want to believe that.

It doesn't hold up very well when you think about it.

If good work is bound to get popular, how do you explain the books and records that go unnoticed for years and then get rediscovered?

In 2006, a group of researchers ran an experiment.

They asked more than ten thousand people to listen to songs by unknown bands.

Some listeners had to decide which songs they liked on their own. Others could see what people before them had downloaded.

When people could see each other's choices, popular songs became more popular. The same set of songs could also end up with very different rankings in different groups.

The songs stayed the same.

What changed was how people came across them and made their choices.

Good songs still had a better chance of being liked. The experiment leaves room for quality to matter.

It also shows how much else can matter.

We've all had some version of that experience.

You finally watch a good film that's been out for years because a friend mentions it in passing.

You start a series you've never heard of because a clip catches your eye.

Or you open a novel for the first time because its author told a joke online.

**Something can be worth liking and never get the chance to be liked.**

The internet has no jury judging each work on its merits.

It feels more like a crowded street.

Everyone's talking, and everyone wants you to stop and look. Someone's singing, someone's arguing, someone's selling something. Someone else is just standing there telling a joke.

Having something good may not be enough to stop people as they walk by.

You may have better luck if they find you interesting first.

## When people start following the person

Think about some of the people you've followed online.

At first you might just think one of their videos is funny.

After a while, you notice they have a particular way of talking.

Then you start to remember stories they've told. You know what they're working on these days. You might even wonder how something they mentioned turned out.

Somewhere along the way, what you're looking for changes.

At first, you came across something they posted.

Now you go looking to see what they've posted lately.

It sounds like a small difference. It changes what you're paying attention to.

If a stranger posts a photo of their dinner, you might scroll right past it.

If it's someone you like, you might stop and look. You might even think, of course that's what they'd have for dinner.

The photo is no more interesting on its own.

**You've become interested in the person who took it.**

That's what I mean by consuming the person.

There's nothing new about the idea. People have long followed actors, singers, and athletes beyond any one film, song, or game.

The internet gives ordinary people a chance to build that kind of relationship too.

You can have people follow you over time without already being famous.

A story from your life, a sense of humor, a strange hobby, or a particular way of looking at the world might be enough to make a stranger curious about you.

The relationship between the work and the person can then go the other way.

At first, people notice you because of something you made.

Later, they give your new work a look because they already know who you are.

Yu Hua is a good example.

Some people got to know him through his books. Others saw a different side of him online and found their way back to the books.

The work helps people discover the person, and the person helps people discover the work.

That brings up another question.

What makes someone worth following over time?

## Why do we want to know what happens to someone next?

Imagine two people.

The first is an excellent programmer. He's good at his job, his life is stable, and work goes much the same from one day to the next.

The second is a good programmer too.

One day, he quits his job to try building products on his own.

A few months later, he has a product and hardly anyone uses it. He starts to doubt his judgment and tries to learn marketing. That brings up a whole new set of problems he hadn't expected.

Whose story would you be more likely to keep following?

Probably the second person's.

Both programmers might be equally good.

With the second, there's something you can't predict.

Why did he do it? What happened next? Did it work out? Will he give up?

Once you start asking those questions, a job title and a few traits no longer tell you everything you want to know about him.

There's a story unfolding.

It made me think of an idea.

**Breaking out of a steady state.**

We're used to making sense of people through labels that stay put.

She's a writer. He's a programmer. This person is serious. That person is rich.

Labels help us get a quick read on someone. They can also make that person seem predictable.

Then they do something you didn't expect, and you have to rethink the picture you had of them.

Yu Hua didn't have to change anything about his life. People just had to discover how funny the author of those heavy novels could be off the page.

Sometimes it's the person's life that changes.

Someone leaves a familiar job to start a business. Someone learns a skill they've never tried before. Someone realizes they might have been wrong about something they believed for years.

Each change raises questions, and those questions give other people a reason to stick around.

I eventually realized that a constant stream of surprises won't do it either.

If someone is different every day and you never get a sense of who they are, it's hard to feel like you know them.

People like surprises. They need some familiarity too.

Think of a good TV series.

You know who the main character is. You know their personality and could probably guess how they'd react if they got into trouble.

You still want to know what happens in the next episode.

**Familiarity helps us remember someone. Change makes us curious about what happens to them next.**

Maybe that's what keeps people following someone over time.

## What can an ordinary person do with this?

Writing this makes me laugh a little.

I'm in the middle of something similar myself.

I've built a few software products lately.

As a developer, I took it for granted that if the features worked well and the experience felt polished, the next thing that would happen was that users would find them.

It turns out there's a long way between building a product and getting someone to use it.

I once tried giving away a two-dollar app for free on social media.

Hardly anyone saw the post.

A regular post introducing the app did better.

So I started looking into recommendation algorithms, pictures, humor, attention, and what makes people want to follow another person.

I ended up somewhere a little ironic.

I'd spent a lot of time wondering how to get people to see what I made. I'd barely thought about why anyone would be interested in me in the first place.

If all I ever talk about is the features I've built, the technology I've used, and the problems I've solved, it might mean very little to someone who doesn't know me.

Things might be different if they gradually got to know me and understood why I made these things, where I got stuck, and what odd ideas I tried along the way.

Staging failures for attention or turning my life into a reality show would miss the point.

The changes I think are worth writing about are the ones that happen on their own.

An experiment that failed.

Something I suddenly changed my mind about.

A small thing that puzzled me.

Or just a funny moment in an ordinary day.

Any one of those things might be too slight for an article on its own.

Over time, they can give people a sense of who you are.

Put those bits of experience together and a story starts to emerge.

## Where I've landed for now

I keep thinking about two things we tend to lump together when we talk about making things online.

One is making something worth people's time.

The other is becoming someone worth following.

The first asks what you gave people today.

The second asks why they'd be interested in what happens to you tomorrow.

The two can go together, in either order.

Some people become known for their work. Other people's work gets discovered because people know them. Often it goes back and forth.

Of course, plenty of people would rather stay out of the public eye. Plenty of good work gets around without its creator having to charm anyone.

For ordinary people who want their work to help them build a following over time, though, it's worth thinking about.

I could spend every day trying to get a little more traffic on the next post. Every so often, I'd rather ask myself this.

**If someone came across me for the first time today, what would make them want to know what happens to me next?**

I don't have a full answer yet.

One thing is clearer to me now.

We tend to assume we have to keep making good things before anyone has a reason to follow us.

Sometimes it happens the other way around.

**People find you interesting first. Then they might discover that what you make is interesting too.**
