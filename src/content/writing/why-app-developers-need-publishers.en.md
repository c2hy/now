*Rethinking software creation, distribution, and business in the age of AI through changes to App Store compliance*

## 1. A problem we have overlooked for a long time

In November 2026, Apple updated how compliance materials are managed in App Store Connect, further clarifying the declaration requirements for features such as AI-generated or synthetic content and security assessments in relevant markets.

The change has put many independent developers under pressure.

To be clear, this does not mean the platform has broadly banned individual developers from publishing apps. Regulatory requirements vary by product, and a security assessment is not a universal prerequisite for every app.

But it has made an existing question harder to ignore: **if one person builds a piece of software, should they also have to bear every responsibility involved in bringing it to market and operating it over time?**

An independent developer often has to do more than write code. They design the product, study app store policies, handle reviews, find users, run social media, manage payments, answer support requests, fix vulnerabilities, handle filings, and deal with whatever legal and financial issues arise.

Some of that work is closely tied to creating software. Some of it belongs to entirely different professions.

We have grown used to this arrangement.

But it is worth asking whether it is natural.

**Why can someone who writes a novel hand publishing, distribution, and marketing to a publisher, while someone who builds an app is often expected to run a software company on their own?**

That may be a question worth revisiting.

---

## 2. The App Store is a bookstore, but not quite a publisher

The software industry has had publishers for a long time.

Video games developed a division between developers and publishers early on. Development teams create the game; publishers take on funding, testing, localization, marketing, and channel management.

But in the mobile internet era, especially in consumer software outside games, the idea of independent publishing became unusually widespread.

The reason is easy to understand.

The App Store dramatically lowered the cost of reaching a market. Developers no longer need to build their own sales networks, negotiate with every retailer, or create a full system for downloads, payments, and automatic updates. As long as their software meets the platform's requirements, an individual developer can offer it to users around the world.

That was a major advance.

But it may have led us to a mistaken conclusion:

**Lowering the barrier to distribution does not eliminate the value of professional publishing.**

A bookstore helps a work reach readers. A publisher has to decide what is worth publishing, help the author improve it, identify its audience, set a price, organize promotion, and take on the related business risk.

The App Store offers more infrastructure than a traditional bookstore, but it still does not do all of that work for every developer.

It can list an app, but it cannot guarantee that anyone will download it. It can provide a payment system, but it cannot make users willing to pay. It can review whether software follows platform rules, but it will not take on all of the developer's legal and business responsibilities. And it will not build a sustainable business for someone simply because they wrote good code.

So there is still a gap between creating software and operating a software business—one app stores have not closed.

Many independent developers have chosen to fill that gap themselves. That does not mean they are the only ones who can.

---

## 3. AI may be changing the basis for the industry's old division of labor

Until recently, software development was relatively expensive. Building a commercial product of any complexity usually required years of learning or a team with several kinds of expertise.

That gave software creation the character of corporate production. Developers had to consider project management, commercial returns, and long-term operations at the same time. Even solo developers often needed strong engineering and business skills.

AI is changing this.

Natural-language programming, coding assistants, automated testing, and code generation make it possible for more people to build software that used to be beyond their reach. This does not mean everyone can build reliable, excellent commercial software. But it does lower one important barrier: **the distance between an idea and a working product.**

As a result, a less common kind of creator is becoming more visible: someone with an idea, a sense of design, or a particular understanding of a need, who can use AI to turn that into software.

But they may not want to run a software company.

Some people enjoy creating tools but dislike marketing. Some are good at product design but do not want to study law and finance. Others simply want their work to reach more people and earn a reasonable income.

**A growing number of creators does not mean the number of software operators has to grow at the same rate.**

That may be one of AI's deeper effects on software. In the past, development skill was the main barrier to entry. In the future, more of the barriers may lie in product judgment, finding a market, earning user trust, ongoing operations, and compliance. Creation is getting easier, but turning a creation into a sustainable economic activity remains difficult.

That is exactly where professional specialization can return.

---

## 4. Compliance changes are not the whole opportunity; they make the tension more visible

Recent changes to app compliance requirements make this separation clearer.

For products with certain features, developers may need to prepare filings, declarations, security assessment documents, and other materials. This creates an obvious economic problem:

Suppose a compliance task requires a fixed amount of work. For a product generating hundreds of thousands of yuan a month, that may be a normal operating expense. For a small tool maintained by one person in their spare time, earning only a few hundred yuan a month, the same cost may be enough to make the developer give up publishing it.

**The same compliance requirement can have very different economic effects on products of different sizes.** That difference does not necessarily reflect the value of the products.

A niche tool may provide irreplaceable help to a few hundred users, yet have too small a market to support a full operating structure on its own. This points to a pattern worth watching:

Some software does have users and value, but **its economics are too small to carry the fixed costs of operating it independently**.

If this situation is widespread, the market may need a new kind of service: **letting several independent works share professional operating capabilities.**

But sharing professional capabilities does not mean sharing every credential. A publishing partnership cannot simply borrow a company identity or developer account to replace real compliance obligations. A viable business would not rent out publishing eligibility in bulk; it would be a software publisher with real operating responsibilities and expertise.

---

## 5. What should a software publisher actually do?

If we call a professional organization an app publisher, its job cannot stop at filling in store listing forms. It has to create value that developers struggle to get while operating independently.

1. **Curation:** Once AI lowers the cost of creation, there may be a flood of software. But being buildable does not mean a product is worth operating over the long term. A publisher needs to judge whether a product meets a real need and how easily it could be replaced. **Publishing is not about putting every work on the market; it is about choosing how to spend limited operating resources.**
2. **Product editing:** A good software publisher should do more than check whether the code runs. It should help with positioning, interaction, pricing, feature choices, and onboarding, helping creators see the product through the eyes of someone who does not already know it.
3. **Distribution and growth:** App store optimization (ASO), content, channel partnerships, paid promotion, and localization. The publisher has to show that it can reach the market better than the creator could alone. Otherwise, it is just adding friction in the middle.
4. **Ongoing operations:** This is where apps differ most from books. Operating system upgrades, changes to third-party services, payment failures, security vulnerabilities, and server outages can all break software. **An app publisher should not only get a product to launch; it should also answer who will be responsible for it three or five years later.**

From this perspective, an app publisher combines parts of a traditional publisher, a record label, and a software operator. It manages both the work and its future.

---

## 6. This is not a brand-new industry, but the market may be changing

Look at the real market and you will find that similar practices already exist:

* Companies such as **Kovalee** offer professional publishing services for consumer apps. Developers focus on the code while the publisher takes on user acquisition, app store optimization, and monetization, often through a revenue-sharing model.
* Companies such as **Codeway** also operate at scale through co-publishing or by acquiring products outright.
* Overseas, there are app aggregators that acquire independent apps and build portfolios by improving monetization and maintaining them over the long term.

The key question is: **could AI make professional publishing capabilities—once aimed mainly at mature products—available to more small, independent creators?**

Traditional publishers are more likely to choose potential hits because publishing requires fixed investment. But if AI gradually lowers the cost of testing, maintenance, analysis, and operations, some works that were previously uneconomic to publish may become viable.

Here is a hypothesis worth testing: **the smallest product a professional publisher can serve is getting smaller.**

As software production gets cheaper, trustworthy curation and ongoing operations may matter more than the ability to get an app listed.

---

## 7. A publisher cannot rely only on charging developers

This is where the idea can go wrong most easily.

When independent developers are worried about compliance and distribution, the most obvious business model is to charge them for services. But in practice, **the developers who need the most help are often the ones least able to pay for it.**

A small app with almost no revenue cannot easily afford expensive compliance advice, publishing management, and ongoing operations.

So a software publisher's long-term income should not mainly come from selling app listing services. It should be built around the works themselves:

* **Co-publishing:** The creator keeps the intellectual property. The publisher receives operating rights for a defined period and shares profits according to the work and risk each side takes on.
* **Acquisition:** If a product has real users but its creator no longer wants to maintain it, the publisher can buy it and take over its operations.

Co-publishing is especially worth considering. It lets creators stay focused on creating while an organization with operating expertise handles the business. But it should not become an unfair form of extraction: the creator does all the development while the publisher only provides an account and takes a large cut indefinitely. A fair partnership should give both sides something they could not get by acting alone.

---

## 8. The real economic problem: a hundred small apps may not be worth more than ten big ones

Suppose a publisher partners with an app that generates ¥2,000 a month in distributable revenue. The publisher gets 30%, or ¥600. That ¥600 has to cover customer support, technical help, and administration. If the product keeps requiring human effort, the publisher may lose money.

Operating a thousand unprofitable small apps does not automatically create economies of scale. A thousand products mean a thousand potential maintenance obligations, user complaints, and security risks.

To scale, a software publisher has to find capabilities it can genuinely share: common quality-testing processes, release systems, standardized support tools, business analytics, and channels for products with similar users. But a product's own legal responsibilities and technical risks do not disappear just because it joins a shared brand.

So the operating logic has to **shift from maximizing the number of works to building a high-quality, manageable portfolio**. The bar for submitting a product can be low, but the bar for publishing it must not be.

---

## 9. Why would creators trust a publisher?

Beyond the business model, there is a basic question: if a developer hands their software to a publisher, what might they lose?

They might lose control over revenue, release authority, or even the ability to decide what happens to the product.

Whether a software publisher can work depends on whether it can create trustworthy agreements:

1. **Clear limits on authorization:** Copyright and commercial operating rights should be distinguished. The author keeps the intellectual property while the publisher receives operating authority within an agreed scope. But with an app, the publisher also needs real control over signing credentials, the build process, and security fixes.
2. **Transparent revenue sharing:** Is the split based on gross revenue or net profit after expenses? Who decides the marketing budget? How are losses shared? The contract needs to make these things clear.
3. **A reasonable exit:** When the partnership ends, can the author regain publishing rights? What happens to historical data and user obligations?

Apple does allow eligible apps to be transferred between developer accounts, but that does not mean every data set, contract, and user obligation can move without conditions.

**A software publisher worth trusting should give creators the freedom not to operate the business themselves without taking away their reasonable rights to their work.**

---

## 10. The Chinese market may be a catalyst, not the whole opportunity

It is easy to draw a narrow conclusion from the compliance changes in one market: if individual developers have trouble publishing, set up a company that helps them “publish under its account.”

But that would amount to operating an intermediary service exposed to policy changes. Policies may shift, review rules may change, and the market could quickly turn into a price war.

More importantly, that would not solve the commercialization problem for independent software. If a publisher helps a hundred apps get listed and ninety of them still have no real users, the long-term value it creates is limited.

Independent developers around the world also struggle with user acquisition, app store policies, payments and taxes, privacy compliance, and monetization. The changes in China are better seen as a window into a broader tension between creation and operations.

Over the long term, a competitive publisher should be able to operate good software across markets.

---

## 11. It could also change how software consumers make choices

If software publishers become more common, they could offer something to consumers as well as creators.

When people buy independent software today, they are taking on a risk: there may be only one developer behind the product. If that person loses interest or changes jobs, the software may slowly become a “zombie app.”

A professional publisher with stable maintenance standards could offer another kind of value: **even if the original author stops working on it day to day, the product can continue to receive support.**

If a publishing brand consistently follows the same product principles, privacy standards, and service commitments, consumers may begin to trust it. That resembles how readers trust a publisher's taste in books, but this kind of trust has to come from the real performance of many products over time.

**What is worth hoping for is not that consumers remember a publisher's name, but that the name gives them a reliable expectation of software quality and continued service.**

---

## 12. This model could create new problems too

A software publisher is not necessarily a better system than independent publishing.

If professional publishers gain control over market channels, user data, and commercial settlements, they could gain more bargaining power. Creators might move from depending on app stores to depending on both app stores and publishers.

And if publishers pursue efficiency by choosing only products that are easy to monetize and standardize, many distinctive niche works could remain excluded. That could create a new set of gatekeepers in the software market.

Professional publishing should therefore be one more option for independent creators, not the only path.

The software ecosystem of the future may not need one uniform organizational model. Some people will keep building and publishing independently; some will work with publishers; others will sell their products to professional operators.

What matters is whether AI lets more people **choose how they want to create and operate**, instead of forcing them to take on every role.

---

## 13. We may be redefining what it means to create software

The arrival of app stores gave individual developers their first chance to reach consumers around the world directly. It also created an ideal: one person makes the product, publishes it, finds users, and keeps all the revenue.

That model is appealing, but it compresses too many different professional skills into one person.

AI is changing this structure. It makes one person's creative capacity stronger, but that does not mean the same person should have to be an engineer, product manager, marketer, lawyer, finance department, and support team.

Perhaps we need to distinguish two roles:

* **Software creators:** people who make digital works that are useful to others.
* **Software operators:** people who take responsibility for bringing a product to market, keeping its promises to users, and earning a return from it.

One person can play both roles, but **they should not have to**.

That may be the most meaningful role for a software publisher. It does not add an unnecessary layer between creators and users. When the professional skills really complement each other, it lets two kinds of work that had been forced together separate again.

If this division of labor works, more people who do not want to run a software company may still earn income by creating good software. We may also see companies focused on discovering, improving, publishing, and operating good software over the long term.

This structure is already established in books, music, and video games. For non-game consumer software, especially the individual works emerging in the age of AI, it is taking on new practical significance.

---

## Conclusion: let creating software become a career people can choose on its own

Higher fixed costs from regulation may create opportunities for new divisions of labor, or they may simply push some small products out of the market. More developers also does not mean more excellent work or more paying users at the same rate.

Whether software publishing can work still comes down to a few plain questions:

Can publishers recognize valuable work?
Can they get better commercial results for it than independent operation would?
Can they take on long-term operating responsibilities at a reasonable cost?
Can creators receive a return while keeping the freedom and rights they deserve?

If these questions have no answers, a “software publisher” is just an app-listing service with a new name. But if it can answer them, the value it offers is much greater than getting an app into a store.

In the past, writing a book did not mean an author had to start a publishing house.

Perhaps in the future, building an app should not mean a developer has to run a software company.

**As AI makes software easier to create, the software industry may need to rethink not only how to produce more software, but how to give more valuable work professional, trustworthy, and sustainable operations.**

That is what a true software publisher should exist to do.
