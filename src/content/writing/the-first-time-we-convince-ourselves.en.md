A while ago I was often working late. Sometimes it was three or four in the morning. My body was exhausted, but my mind kept turning over how a feature should work. Lying down didn't help: the problem was still there, and the day didn't feel finished. The phone screen was still lit. I picked it up again and went back to the part I hadn't finished.

Every time, I had an excuse: "it's almost done, just tying up the loose ends," "I'll sleep better once this is solved," "it's only one last little thing." On their own, each one sounded reasonable. After saying it, I filled a glass of water and sat back down at the computer.

Then a few days ago my body started to ache and feel tight. I realized I hadn't looked at my routine in a long time.

I went back through some earlier notes, to see whether this had happened before. The times in those notes got later and later, almost all of them past midnight. That's when I noticed something: the times I found it hardest to stop were also the times I was most anxious.

At first I assumed I was just busy. But plenty of it wasn't urgent enough to need solving at four in the morning. Looked at the next day, finishing a few hours earlier would have changed almost nothing.

And still, once night came, I couldn't stop.

What I couldn't stand, I realized later, was things being unresolved. A feature unfinished, a question unanswered—I wanted it closed as fast as possible, as if solving it tonight would make the hovering feeling go away too. So sometimes it wasn't the work that kept me going. It was the hovering feeling. I didn't notice that at the time.

If it had happened once, I wouldn't have thought much of it. Working until four could be an occasional deadline push. Anxiety could be something on my mind that day. Exhaustion was nothing unusual. In the morning I washed my face and went out as usual. Each one on its own had a reasonable explanation. What bothered me was seeing them together.

And this wasn't the first time. The scattered notes had seemed unrelated, but when the same state showed up again, I recognized it at once: I'd been here before—four in the morning, anxious, giving myself another reason to keep going.

That made me rethink what records are for.

I used to think records were mainly so I'd remember later—what I'd done, how I'd felt. But more and more I think most records mean little at the moment they're written. Anxious today, up late last night, suddenly exhausted one afternoon—on their own, none of it proves much; it might just be a bad night's sleep. Some entries were one line. Some were not even that—just a timestamp. No need to analyze it right away. Leave it there first.

What matters is usually not any single entry. It's noticing, some time later, that the same thing has come back. Only then can I confirm it: so it wasn't an accident.

A record can tell me it came back. It can't tell me why. That still takes a separate look.
