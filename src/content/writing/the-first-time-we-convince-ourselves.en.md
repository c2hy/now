A while ago, I was often working late. Sometimes it would be three or four in the morning. My body was clearly exhausted, but my mind kept turning, trying to figure out how a feature should actually work. Lying down didn’t really help; the problem was still hanging there, and I felt as if the day hadn’t truly ended.

Every time I stayed up late, I could come up with a fairly reasonable explanation—“It’s almost done; I’ll just tie up the loose ends,” “I’ll sleep more soundly once I solve this,” “It’s only one last little thing.” Taken on their own, none of these explanations seemed unreasonable.

It was only a few days ago, when my body started to feel sore and tense, that I suddenly realized I had gone a long time without seriously looking at my routine.

So I went back through some of my earlier notes. That was when I noticed something I had never been aware of before: the times when I found it especially hard to stop seemed often to come with anxiety.

At first, I thought I was simply too busy. But the more I thought about it, the less complete that explanation seemed. Some things were nowhere near urgent enough to need solving at four in the morning. In fact, when I looked at them the next day, finishing them a few hours earlier the night before made almost no difference to finishing them that morning.

And yet, once night came, I found it very hard to stop.

Only gradually did I realize that what I might actually be unable to tolerate was the fact that something was still unresolved. A feature wasn’t finished, a question still had no answer, something was left hanging—and I would want to close it out as quickly as possible. It was as if solving it that night would make the uncertainty disappear too. So sometimes what kept me working wasn’t the work itself, but the fact that I was already being pushed along by my emotions—I just didn’t notice it at the time.

If this had happened only once, I probably wouldn’t have thought much of it. Working until four could be explained as an occasional push to meet a deadline; anxiety could be explained by having something on my mind that day; being physically exhausted didn’t seem especially meaningful—each thing, considered on its own, had a reasonable explanation.

What made me feel that something was slightly off was putting them together.

I realized this kind of situation wasn’t happening for the first time. The scattered notes I’d left behind before hadn’t seemed connected. But when the same state appeared again, I suddenly recognized it: this wasn’t the first time I’d been like this.

Because of that, I’ve been rethinking what it means to keep a record.

I used to think the main purpose of recording things was to remember later where I’d been, what I’d done, and how I’d felt at the time. But I’m increasingly convinced that many records have no special meaning at the moment they’re written. Feeling anxious today doesn’t necessarily mean anything. Staying up late last night doesn’t necessarily mean anything. Suddenly feeling exhausted on a particular day may really just mean I didn’t sleep well. We don’t even need to analyze it right away. We can leave it there first.

Because what may really matter isn’t what happened on any one particular occasion, but that some time later, you notice it happening again—and only then do you get your first chance to realize that it wasn’t an accident after all.
